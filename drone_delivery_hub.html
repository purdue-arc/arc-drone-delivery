What this project is

A drone that delivers packages without ever landing at the customer's address. It flies to the delivery point, hovers, lowers the package on a winch, reels the cable back in, and flies home. It only ever lands at its own base, on a printed marker called an AprilTagA high-contrast printed square pattern, like a chunky QR code. The onboard camera uses it to work out exactly how far away the pad is and at what angle., which its camera uses to line itself up precisely.

Everything below is written from the software's own change log and the team's own review documents. Nothing here is invented or estimated.

Is it flying yet?

Not on real hardware yet.

The complete delivery mission runs start to finish in simulationA computer model of the aircraft and the world (using a physics engine called Gazebo) that the real flight software flies against, so bugs are found before anything leaves the ground.. Nothing in it has ever run on the physical aircraft. See the Status tab for exactly what's proven and what's still open.

The five-second mental model

A small onboard computer (a JetsonThe onboard computer carried by the aircraft. It runs the flight software (ROS 2) and talks to the flight controller below it.) runs the mission logic in a framework called ROS 2The software framework the onboard code is built on. Individual programs are called "nodes" and they talk to each other over named "topics.". It never flies the aircraft directly. It sends movement requests to a flight controller board running firmware called PX4The flight controller firmware. It is the only thing with direct authority over the motors, and it always keeps the final say. If a pilot takes manual control, the mission software goes silent., which is the only thing with direct authority over the motors and which also supplies the aircraft's own position, trusted completely, from GPS. A lidar sensor builds a live map of obstacles; a route planner called Nav2The route-planning software that finds a path around obstacles using the live obstacle map. It only plans. The mission software does the actual flying. finds a way around them. The newest addition is FAST-LIO2A lidar-inertial SLAM system that gives the aircraft's position an independent second opinion, fusing the same lidar points with an inertial sensor rather than trusting GPS. It logs how far it disagrees with the flight controller and, only if enabled, can nudge the obstacle map to correct for that drift. It never sends commands to the motors and is switched off by default., which cross-checks that GPS position against the lidar instead of just trusting it; it runs alongside the mission, never touches the flight controls, and is off by default until it has flown enough to be trusted. That's the whole stack.

Can we fly this on the aircraft?

Not yet.

The software runs the complete mission in simulation, including a full delivery on the hardest route flown so far (596 m, obstacles on both legs). Nothing in it has ever run on real hardware. One especially dangerous failure mode has been found and closed. It is described below.

The mid-air disarm risk is resolved

Closed, 2026-09-02 (late night)

A stalled descent used to be able to cut the motors while the aircraft was still in the air, if the height estimate had drifted. It now hands the last metre to PX4's own automatic landing instead of disarming. Verified in simulation with zero self-disarms. See the Timeline for the full story.

Fall 2026 Goals

Project vision: a reliable, functional drone delivery system on Purdue's campus that delivers food and packages autonomously, engineered within safety, airspace and campus constraints to reduce congestion on walkways and roads, navigate urban environments, and deliver payloads up to 3.5 kg with precision. The aircraft uses lidar, GPS, stereo vision and an IMU to take off, navigate to a delivery location, and land precisely and safely.

Progress below is assessed only from what's actually in CHANGELOG.md and the linked artifacts. Hardware

What's actually been built

The hardware team builds the airframes and the ground systems around them, including chargers, the parts inventory, and the wiring that links the flight controller to the onboard computer. This section is condensed from the team's own Notion hardware docs, so it names real parts and real problems, not a generic parts list. Before anyone flies any of this, see the Flight PrepThe team's before-you-fly safety and legal checklist. It has its own tab, not repeated here. tab.

The airframes

v1 DroneIn use

The team's first package-capable drone, built on a Tarot T960 hexacopter frame, the widest hexacopter frame Tarot sells. This is the airframe in the photos below. Its main flight risk is an ESC or motor failure, which gives no warning and pulls the drone hard to one side. The standard response is to flip the kill switch immediately.

Test DroneIn use

A simple red-and-white quadcopter, not built for delivery. Used for practice flights and controller checkout. Its GPS receiver is particularly weak. Expect to need an open area and some patience for a lock.

v2 DroneResearch / costed

A "production-level mechanical hardware" study, not a build: a larger frame (T960 again, or an octacopter past 1 m diameter), professional Hobbywing/T-Motor motors and ESCs for failover diagnostics, and a from-scratch parachute/airbag safety system. Rough budget from the team's own costing: about $4,600.

v3 DroneConcept

The production-drone target: fully sealed, redundant (2x) critical systems, a custom frame and possibly a custom flight-controller PCB. Still mostly unscoped. Whether to keep the Pixhawk or build a custom flight computer is an open question.

The layers

Frame & propulsion STRUCTURE

T960 hexacopter frame on v1 (Tarot's widest hex frame); a simple quadcopter frame on the Test Drone. Spare props, prop mounts, and carbon-fiber offcuts are kept in the parts box for repairs.

Power system BATTERY & DISTRIBUTION

3S 5200 mAh battery on the Test Drone, 6S on the newer build; a power module steps the ~11 V battery rail down for the flight controller and motors, and a power-distribution board fans it out to each ESC. Always balance-charge any battery with more than one cell. The team charges on an ONYX 235, which auto-configures once you set cell count and capacity.

Compute & flight-control link JETSON ↔ PIXHAWK

The Jetson talks to the Pixhawk 6C over UART on TELEM2 (Jetson GPIO pins 6/8/10, device /dev/ttyTHS1), bridged by a uXRCE-DDS agentThe current bridge between PX4's internal topics and ROS 2. It replaced an older bridge called microRTPS, which was deprecated in PX4 1.14 and needed custom firmware. The current agent doesn't. running on the Jetson. It's the modern replacement for the deprecated microRTPS bridge, and it needs no custom PX4 firmware. Direct UART is the first thing to try; USB FTDI is the documented fallback if it doesn't come up.

Camera & perception ZED 2i

A ZED 2i stereo camera feeds the Jetson over USB. An Intel RealSense was tried first and rejected. See Known Issues below. Live video leaves the Jetson over GStreamer/UDP rather than the ZED SDK's own streaming sample, which the Jetson Orin's hardware can't run.

Gimbal CAMERA STABILIZATION

RCTimer gimbal motors and controller board, built from an Instructables brushless-gimbal guide. Pitch is driven by RC input wired from the flight controller's PWM output into the gimbal controller's RC ports; the controller runs on its own 2S–4S battery, separate from the main pack.

Payload & delivery CONCEPT STAGE

The delivery mechanism itself, whether winch or drop, is still being proven out on the v2 or v3 airframe. One candidate hardware option under consideration for the release hook is a commercial latch-and-release mechanism rather than a fully custom part.

Comms TELEMETRY & RADIO

A SiK telemetry radio links the Pixhawk to QGroundControl on the ground; a separate RC radio handles manual flight. Longer-range options are still research: a cellular modem kit for LTE telemetry, and WiFi HaLow, either bridged through a phone or daisy-chained between the lab and the flying field, for getting drone data onto the internet without relying on a cell connection.

How the pieces connect

Bring-up in short: flash the Jetson with a Humble-compatible Ubuntu 22.04 image, install ROS 2 Humble, wire TELEM2 to the GPIO UART pins above, then in QGroundControl point UXRCE_DDS_CFG at TELEM2 and set SER_TEL2_BAUD to 921600. Build and run the Micro-XRCE-DDS agent on the Jetson, and PX4's uORB topics show up as ROS 2 topics on the other side. No custom firmware is needed, unlike the old microRTPS-based guide this replaced.

Parts on hand with known issues, don't reach for these

  • One spare ESC is shorted. The one missing its XT60 connector has a short circuit. Do not use until repaired.
  • The spare Tarot power distribution board doesn't fit. It takes XT90 connectors, not the XT60 the v1 build needs. A different board was bought for v1. The Tarot board is being kept for a possible future drone.
  • The Remote ID unit in the Pixhawk box was a mistake purchase. It still requires separate FAA device registration to be legal to use. Buy an officially FAA-registered product (e.g. Dronetag) instead.
  • The spare GPS module is lower quality. The Test Drone's built-in GPS is already unreliable on its own; don't expect a quick lock.
  • The Intel RealSense camera was evaluated and rejected for obstacle-avoidance depth sensing. It had ~1 m effective range and poor image quality. The ZED 2i replaced it.
  • Spare 40 A ESCs are undersized. The current build needs 50 A; the 40 A units are backups only.

Flight Prep

Condensed from the team's own “Before You Fly” and operations docs. Read the full originals in Notion before actually flying; this is an orientation summary, not the complete legal or safety guidance.

Every pilot needs: FAA drone & pilot registration, the TRUST certificate, and to have read the team's full safety doc.

Purdue has a blanket approval for club educational/research flights on campus property, per the university's risk management office, and club flights carry liability and property-damage coverage. That doesn't replace federal rules: check air.aloft.ai for current airspace restrictions before every flight, and get LAANC authorization (via airspacelink.com) if flying in controlled airspace. Uncontrolled airspace doesn't require it. Flying beyond visual line of sight or for package delivery each have their own separate FAA paperwork.

Before every flight

  • Frame and propellers show no damage; all wiring is shielded, strapped down, and covered.
  • Every connector is locked or highly resistant to coming loose. Avoid connectors like USB for anything load-bearing.
  • Failsafes are configured sensibly for where you're actually flying (e.g. low-battery return-to-home won't land it on something unstable).
  • Registration and TRUST certificates are on hand.
  • Airspace checked on air.aloft.ai; LAANC obtained if required.
  • Weather checked. No flying in high wind, rain, snow, or drizzle; nothing on this airframe is water-resistant beyond the motors.

During every flight

  • Always fly with a second person watching telemetry and surroundings.
  • Maintain visual line of sight and keep well clear of people, trees, and other obstacles.
  • Monitor battery voltage in QGroundControl. Don't rely solely on automatic return-to-home.
  • Stay within the authorized LAANC area and altitude limit.
  • Be ready on the kill switch at all times.
